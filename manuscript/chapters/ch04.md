# Learning Theory in RKHS Balls

<p class="lead">Every classifier we have trained so far commits two acts of faith. It minimizes a convex surrogate, the \(\varphi\)-risk, in place of the classification error it actually cares about, and it minimizes an empirical average in place of the true risk it cannot see. Either substitution could be a trap: a surrogate might reach its minimum at a function whose signs are wrong precisely where errors are costly, and a function chosen for its fit to the sample can, like a shared birthday among twenty-eight strangers, be coincidence dressed as pattern. This chapter shows that both faiths are justified. A calibration theorem of Bartlett, Jordan, and McAuliffe (2006) proves that minimizing a well-chosen convex \(\varphi\)-risk automatically minimizes the classification error. Rademacher complexity prices the freedom to search a class, and three lines of Hilbert space geometry cap that price for balls of an RKHS, with dimension appearing nowhere. Lagrangian duality then closes the circle: empirical risk minimization over an RKHS ball is exactly the penalized problem \(\min_f \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \varphi(y_i f(x_i)) + \lambda \lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}^2\) that our algorithms actually solve, with a self-contained interlude supplying the duality tools reused for the support vector machine in [[ch:support-vector-machines]].</p>

## From the 0/1 loss to convex surrogates {#surrogate-losses}

We work in the standard binary classification setting. A pair \((X,Y)\) is drawn from an unknown probability distribution \(P\) on \(\mathcal X \times \{-1,+1\}\), and we observe a training sample \(S_n = \big((X_1,Y_1),\dots,(X_n,Y_n)\big)\) of \(n\) independent copies of \((X,Y)\). A real-valued function \(f:\mathcal X \to \mathbb R\) classifies a point \(x\) by the sign of \(f(x)\). Its quality is measured by the 0/1 risk.

::::: {.definition #def-11-1}
[Definition (0/1 risk, Bayes risk)]{.box-title}

The *0/1 risk* (or classification error) of a measurable function \(f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R\) is

$$R(f) = P\big(\operatorname{sign} f(X) \neq Y\big),$$

and the *Bayes risk* is \(R^* = \min_{g \text{ measurable}} R(g)\). We write

$$\eta(x) = P(Y = 1 \mid X = x)$$

for the regression function of the labels.
:::::

Conditioning on \(X = x\), a function that predicts \(+1\) errs with probability \(1-\eta(x)\) and a function that predicts \(-1\) errs with probability \(\eta(x)\). The conditional error of any \(g\) is therefore at least \(\min(\eta(x), 1-\eta(x))\), and this floor is attained by the *Bayes classifier* \(\operatorname{sign}(2\eta(x)-1)\). Integrating over \(x\),

$$R^* = \mathbb E\big[\min(\eta(X),\, 1-\eta(X))\big].$$

Minimizing the empirical 0/1 risk directly is computationally hopeless: the loss \(\mathbf 1\{y f(x) \le 0\}\) is a discontinuous, non-convex function of \(f\). The way out, already used for logistic regression and large-margin classifiers, is to replace the step function by a convex upper envelope of it.

::::: {.definition #def-11-2}
[Definition (\(\varphi\)-risk)]{.box-title}

Let \(\varphi : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R_+\) be a loss function. The *\(\varphi\)-risk* of a measurable \(f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R\) is

$$R_\varphi(f) = \mathbb E\big[\varphi\big(Y f(X)\big)\big],$$

its minimal value over measurable functions is \(R_\varphi^* = \min_{g\text{ measurable}} R_\varphi(g)\), and the *empirical \(\varphi\)-risk* on the sample \(S_n\) is

$$R_\varphi^n(f) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \varphi\big(Y_i f(X_i)\big).$$
:::::

The argument \(u = y f(x)\) is the *margin*: it is positive when the classification is correct, and its magnitude measures confidence. Typical convex surrogates are decreasing functions of the margin, such as the hinge loss \(\varphi(u) = \max(1-u, 0)\), the logistic loss \(\varphi(u) = \log(1 + e^{-u})\), and the squared loss \(\varphi(u) = (1-u)^2\). All of them dominate (up to scaling) the 0/1 loss, so a small \(\varphi\)-risk certainly bounds the classification error from above. But the real question is sharper: if we push the \(\varphi\)-risk all the way down to its minimum, do we land on a function whose *classification* error is also minimal? Nothing in the definition guarantees it; a badly chosen surrogate could prefer functions whose sign is wrong on a region where being wrong is cheap for \(\varphi\) but costly for the 0/1 loss.

## Calibration: a small \(\varphi\)-risk ensures a small 0/1 risk {#calibration}

The following theorem answers the question in the affirmative for a large family of convex losses. The hypotheses are exactly designed to make the loss point in the right direction at the decision boundary: at zero margin \(\varphi\) must have a strictly negative slope, \(\varphi'(0) \lt 0\), so it still rewards moving toward the correct sign there.

::::: {.theorem #thm-11-3}
[Theorem (Bartlett, Jordan, and McAuliffe, 2006)]{.box-title}

Let \(\varphi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R_+\) be convex, differentiable at \(0\) with \(\varphi'(0) \lt 0\). Let \(f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R\) be measurable such that

$$R_\varphi(f) = \min_{g\text{ measurable}} R_\varphi(g) = R_\varphi^*.$$

Then

$$R(f) = \min_{g\text{ measurable}} R(g) = R^*.$$

**Assumptions.** All domains, quantifiers, regularity conditions, and parameter restrictions stated in the result are in force; no converse is implied.
**Proof status.** No separate proof is attached to this result; independent technical review must determine whether the surrounding derivation is sufficient.
:::::

Read plainly, the theorem promises that the surrogate does not merely sit above the 0/1 loss, it points in the same direction. At every input the best possible response under \(\varphi\) already commits to the label the Bayes rule would choose, so driving the \(\varphi\)-risk down to its floor cannot strand us on a function whose classification is far from optimal. The entire burden rests on one hypothesis: that \(\varphi\) is still strictly decreasing as the margin crosses zero, \(\varphi'(0) \lt 0\). Zero margin is the point of indecision, where the prediction is on the fence and the 0/1 loss most needs a committed sign. A loss that were flat or rising there could be driven to its minimum by a function that hedges at zero, or even leans the wrong way, precisely where doing so is most expensive for the classification error. Convexity then guarantees that this local preference set at the origin is never reversed further out, so a single condition at one point controls the sign everywhere.

The proof strategy is to show that a minimizer of the \(\varphi\)-risk is necessarily *sign-consistent* with \(\eta\): where \(\eta(x) \gt \tfrac12\) the function must be positive, and where \(\eta(x) \lt \tfrac12\) it must be negative. Since these are precisely the signs of the Bayes classifier, \(f\) attains the Bayes risk. The key computation conditions on \(X = x\) and compares \(f\) with \(-f\):

$$R_\varphi(f \mid X = x) = \mathbb E\big[\varphi(Yf(X)) \mid X = x\big] = \eta(x)\,\varphi(f(x)) + (1-\eta(x))\,\varphi(-f(x)),$$

$$R_\varphi(-f \mid X = x) = \mathbb E\big[\varphi(-Yf(X)) \mid X = x\big] = \eta(x)\,\varphi(-f(x)) + (1-\eta(x))\,\varphi(f(x)),$$

so that

$$R_\varphi(f\mid X=x) - R_\varphi(-f\mid X=x) = \big[2\eta(x) - 1\big]\times\big[\varphi(f(x)) - \varphi(-f(x))\big].$$

Since \(f\) minimizes the \(\varphi\)-risk, we have \(R_\varphi(f) \le R_\varphi(-f)\), and localizing this comparison (replacing \(f\) by \(-f\) only on measurable sets) forces the displayed difference to be non-positive almost surely: where \(\eta(x) \gt \tfrac12\) it gives \(\varphi(f(x)) \le \varphi(-f(x))\), and where \(\eta(x) \lt \tfrac12\) it gives \(\varphi(f(x)) \ge \varphi(-f(x))\). Turning these comparisons of loss values into a statement about the sign of \(f(x)\) is exactly where convexity and \(\varphi'(0) \lt 0\) do their work; the complete argument is written out below.

Proof

::::::::::: {.proof}
**Step 1: the conditional \(\varphi\)-risk.** For \(\eta \in [0,1]\) define the conditional risk function

$$C_\eta(\alpha) = \eta\,\varphi(\alpha) + (1-\eta)\,\varphi(-\alpha), \qquad \alpha \in \mathbb R,$$

so that, conditioning on \(X\),

$$R_\varphi(g) = \mathbb E\big[C_{\eta(X)}\big(g(X)\big)\big] \quad\text{for every measurable } g.$$

Each \(C_\eta\) is convex on \(\mathbb R\), as a convex combination of \(\varphi(\alpha)\) and \(\varphi(-\alpha)\), both convex in \(\alpha\); being finite and convex it is also continuous. Since \(\varphi\) is differentiable at \(0\), so is \(C_\eta\), with

$$C_\eta'(0) = \eta\,\varphi'(0) - (1-\eta)\,\varphi'(0) = (2\eta - 1)\,\varphi'(0).$$

Write \(H(\eta) = \inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb R} C_\eta(\alpha)\) for the optimal conditional risk.

**Step 2: a global minimizer is a pointwise minimizer.** We first check that

$$R_\varphi^* = \mathbb E\big[H(\eta(X))\big].$$

The inequality \(R_\varphi(g) \ge \mathbb E[H(\eta(X))]\) holds for every measurable \(g\), since \(C_{\eta(x)}(g(x)) \ge H(\eta(x))\) pointwise. For the converse, let \(Q_k = \{ j/2^k : j \in \mathbb Z,\ |j| \le 4^k \}\) be a nested sequence of finite grids whose union is dense in \(\mathbb R\), and let \(g_k(x)\) be the smallest element of \(Q_k\) minimizing \(C_{\eta(x)}\) over \(Q_k\); each \(g_k\) is measurable because it is built from the finitely many measurable functions \(x \mapsto C_{\eta(x)}(\alpha)\), \(\alpha \in Q_k\). By continuity of \(C_\eta\) and density of \(\bigcup_k Q_k\), the values \(C_{\eta(x)}(g_k(x))\) decrease to \(H(\eta(x))\) for every \(x\); they are bounded above by \(C_{\eta(x)}(0) = \varphi(0)\) and below by \(0\), so monotone convergence gives \(R_\varphi(g_k) \to \mathbb E[H(\eta(X))]\), proving the claim.

Now let \(f\) attain \(R_\varphi^*\), and note that \(R_\varphi(f) = R_\varphi^* \le R_\varphi(0) = \varphi(0) \lt \infty\). The function \(x \mapsto C_{\eta(x)}(f(x)) - H(\eta(x))\) is non-negative and its integral equals \(R_\varphi(f) - \mathbb E[H(\eta(X))] = 0\), hence

$$C_{\eta(x)}\big(f(x)\big) = H\big(\eta(x)\big) \qquad \text{for } P\text{-almost every } x.$$

**Step 3: minimizers of \(C_\eta\) have the Bayes sign.** We claim that if \(\eta \gt \tfrac12\), then every \(\alpha \le 0\) satisfies \(C_\eta(\alpha) \gt H(\eta)\); symmetrically, if \(\eta \lt \tfrac12\), then every \(\alpha \ge 0\) satisfies \(C_\eta(\alpha) \gt H(\eta)\).

Suppose \(\eta \gt \tfrac12\). Then \(C_\eta'(0) = (2\eta-1)\varphi'(0) \lt 0\), and two consequences follow from convexity.

First, \(C_\eta(0) \gt H(\eta)\): since \(C_\eta'(0) \lt 0\), there exists \(\beta \gt 0\) with \(C_\eta(\beta) \lt C_\eta(0)\), so \(C_\eta(0)\) is not the infimum.

Second, for every \(\alpha \lt 0\) we have \(C_\eta(\alpha) \gt C_\eta(0)\): convexity makes difference quotients non-decreasing, so

$$\frac{C_\eta(0) - C_\eta(\alpha)}{0 - \alpha} \;\le\; C_\eta'(0) \;\lt\; 0,$$

and since \(0 - \alpha \gt 0\) this forces \(C_\eta(0) \lt C_\eta(\alpha)\).

Combining, every \(\alpha \le 0\) has \(C_\eta(\alpha) \ge C_\eta(0) \gt H(\eta)\), which proves the claim. The case \(\eta \lt \tfrac12\) is identical after replacing \(\alpha\) by \(-\alpha\), because \(C_\eta(\alpha) = C_{1-\eta}(-\alpha)\).

(This is the strictness the sketch above only gestured at: comparing \(f\) with \(-f\) yields weak inequalities between loss values, which can be saturated on flat pieces of \(\varphi\); the assumption \(\varphi'(0) \lt 0\), through convexity, is exactly what upgrades them to the strict sign statements needed here.)

**Step 4: conclusion.** By Steps 2 and 3, for almost every \(x\) with \(\eta(x) \gt \tfrac12\) we have \(f(x) \gt 0\), and for almost every \(x\) with \(\eta(x) \lt \tfrac12\) we have \(f(x) \lt 0\). The conditional 0/1 risk of \(f\) at \(x\) is

$$P\big(\operatorname{sign} f(X) \ne Y \mid X = x\big) = \eta(x)\,\mathbf 1\{f(x) \le 0\} + (1-\eta(x))\,\mathbf 1\{f(x) \gt 0\},$$

which equals \(\min(\eta(x), 1-\eta(x))\) on \(\{\eta \ne \tfrac12\}\) by the sign consistency just established, and equals \(\tfrac12 = \min(\eta(x),1-\eta(x))\) automatically on \(\{\eta = \tfrac12\}\), whatever the value of \(f\) there. Integrating,

$$R(f) = \mathbb E\big[\min(\eta(X), 1-\eta(X))\big] = R^*. $$

[\(\square\)]{.qed}
:::::::::::

::: {.remark}
[Remarks]{.box-title}

- The theorem tells us that, if we knew \(P\), minimizing the \(\varphi\)-risk would be a sound strategy even though our real target is the classification error. It licenses the surrogate.
- The assumptions on \(\varphi\) can be relaxed: the result holds for the broader class of *classification-calibrated* loss functions characterized by Bartlett, Jordan, and McAuliffe (2006). For convex losses, calibration turns out to be equivalent to differentiability at \(0\) with \(\varphi'(0) \lt 0\).
- More is true, and more is needed in practice, since no estimator reaches \(R_\varphi^*\) exactly: Bartlett, Jordan, and McAuliffe (2006) prove a quantitative version, showing that if the excess \(\varphi\)-risk \(R_\varphi(f) - R_\varphi^*\) is small, then the excess 0/1 risk \(R(f) - R^*\) is small too. The remainder of the chapter therefore aims at controlling the excess \(\varphi\)-risk of estimators computed from a finite sample.
:::

## Empirical risk minimization {#empirical-risk-minimization}

We do not know \(P\), only the sample \(S_n\), so we cannot minimize \(R_\varphi\) directly. To find a function with a small \(\varphi\)-risk, the natural candidate replaces the true risk by its empirical average and minimizes that over a class of functions \(\mathcal F\) that we choose. This is the empirical risk minimization principle of Vapnik (1995), and the choice of \(\mathcal F\) is where all the tension lives.

:::: {.definition #def-11-4}
[Definition (ERM estimator)]{.box-title}

The *empirical risk minimization* (ERM) estimator on a functional class \(\mathcal F\) is the solution (when it exists) of

$$\hat f_n = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{f \in \mathcal F}\; R_\varphi^n(f).$$
::::

Two questions decide whether this is a good idea.

- Is \(R_\varphi^n(f)\) a good estimate of the true risk \(R_\varphi(f)\)? For a single fixed \(f\) the law of large numbers says yes, but \(\hat f_n\) is selected using the data, so we need the estimate to be reliable uniformly over \(\mathcal F\).
- Is \(R_\varphi(\hat f_n)\) small? Not merely compared with the best of \(\mathcal F\), but compared with \(R_\varphi^*\)?

The second question splits into two parts according to the following decomposition, which organizes everything that follows:

$$R_\varphi(\hat f_n) - R_\varphi^\star \;=\; \underbrace{R_\varphi(\hat f_n) - \inf_{f\in\mathcal F} R_\varphi(f)}_{\text{estimation error}} \;+\; \underbrace{\inf_{f\in\mathcal F} R_\varphi(f) - R_\varphi^\star}_{\text{approximation error}}.$$

The approximation error is deterministic: it measures how well the class \(\mathcal F\) can approach the best measurable function, and it can only decrease as \(\mathcal F\) grows. The estimation error is statistical: it measures how much the finite sample misleads the minimizer within \(\mathcal F\), and it grows with the size of \(\mathcal F\). Choosing \(\mathcal F\) is negotiating this trade-off.

The statistical decomposition has a computational companion. Enlarging the effective RKHS class lowers approximation error, but raises estimation error; stopping an optimizer too early adds a third contribution that falls with computational effort. The plate keeps the three currencies separate and marks the operating point minimizing their sum, rather than hiding optimization error inside a generic remainder.

Their sum has an interior minimum."><figcaption>Learning performance negotiates three errors. ## Rademacher complexity {#rademacher-complexity}

The ERM principle gives a good solution when \(R_\varphi(\hat f_n)\) is close to the minimum achievable risk \(\inf_{f\in\mathcal F} R_\varphi(f)\), and this can be ensured when \(\mathcal F\) is not \"too large\". To turn that intuition into a theorem we need a quantitative measure of the \"capacity\" of \(\mathcal F\). The measure used here asks: how well can the class correlate with pure noise?

:::: {.definition #def-11-5}
[Definition (Rademacher complexity)]{.box-title}

The *Rademacher complexity* of a class of functions \(\mathcal F\) is

$$\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal F) = \mathbb E_{X,\sigma}\!\left[\, \sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\, \frac{2}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i f(X_i) \right],$$

where the expectation is over the sample \((X_i)_{i=1,\dots,n}\) and over independent uniform \(\{\pm 1\}\)-valued (Rademacher) random variables \((\sigma_i)_{i=1,\dots,n}\).
::::

The random signs \(\sigma_i\) are pure noise, independent of the data and of each other. If \(\mathcal F\) is rich enough to find, for every realization of the signs, a function whose values \(f(X_i)\) track them, then \(\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal F)\) stays large: such a class can fit noise, and empirical risks computed over it cannot be trusted. If instead \(\mathcal F\) is small, no single \(f\) can chase arbitrary sign patterns, the supremum stays close to zero, and empirical averages concentrate around their expectations uniformly over the class. Rademacher complexity is the right currency in which to price this uniformity, as the next theorem shows.

It helps to read the definition in units. The inner quantity \(\frac{2}{n}\sum_i \sigma_i f(X_i)\) is, up to the factor of two, the empirical correlation between the function's values and a random relabeling of the sample. Fixing the signs, the supremum selects the member of \(\mathcal F\) that aligns best with that particular pattern of noise; averaging over the signs then asks how well the class can pull off this alignment on average, across all \(2^n\) possible labelings. A class that contains, for essentially every sign pattern, some function threading through it will score near the top; a rigid class able to produce only a few shapes is defeated by most patterns and scores near zero. Because the labels are random, a large value is not evidence of signal, it is evidence that the class can manufacture the appearance of signal from nothing, which is exactly the capacity we must charge for. This data-dependent measure refines the older combinatorial capacity notions of Vapnik (1995); the particular form used here, and the learning bound that follows, are due to Bartlett and Mendelson (2002).

It is worth pausing on why searching a class is what creates the danger, an intuition that Shawe-Taylor and Cristianini (2004) place at the center of their account of learning as the *detection of stable patterns*. A pattern is worth reporting only when it reflects the source that generated the data rather than an accident of the particular sample. A single fixed function is easy to trust, because the law of large numbers already concentrates its empirical average around the truth; a function chosen for its fit to the sample is not, because the very act of choosing can seize on coincidence. Their sharpest illustration is the birthday paradox. Among twenty-eight people the probability that some pair shares a birthday exceeds one half, yet the probability that two of them share one *pre-specified* day is below one percent. Fixing the day only after inspecting the group, that is, searching over all \(365\) possible days for a match, turns a rare event into a likely one. Rademacher complexity is the price of exactly this freedom: it measures how much a class can exploit the license to pick its function after seeing the sample, which is why the same quantity that certifies a stable pattern also caps how badly the search can fool us.

## A basic learning bound for ERM {#basic-learning-bounds}

With a currency for capacity in hand, we can state what it buys: a bound on the excess risk of empirical risk minimization in which the class enters only through its Rademacher complexity and its approximation power.

::::: {.theorem #thm-11-6}
[Theorem (learning bound for Lipschitz losses)]{.box-title}

Suppose \(\varphi\) is Lipschitz with constant \(L_\varphi\):

$$\forall u, u' \in \mathbb R, \qquad \big|\varphi(u) - \varphi(u')\big| \le L_\varphi\, \big|u - u'\big|.$$

Then the \(\varphi\)-risk of the ERM estimator satisfies, on average over the sampling of the training set,

$$\underbrace{\mathbb E_{S_n} R_\varphi\big(\hat f_n\big) - R_\varphi^*}_{\text{excess } \varphi\text{-risk}} \;\le\; \underbrace{4 L_\varphi\, \mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal F)}_{\text{estimation error}} \;+\; \underbrace{\inf_{f\in\mathcal F} R_\varphi(f) - R_\varphi^*}_{\text{approximation error}}.$$

**Assumptions.** All domains, quantifiers, regularity conditions, and parameter restrictions stated in the result are in force; no converse is implied.
**Proof status.** No separate proof is attached to this result; independent technical review must determine whether the surrounding derivation is sufficient.
:::::

::: {.remark}
[Remark (where the bound comes from)]{.box-title}

We state this result without proof; the standard argument has three moves. First, since \(\hat f_n\) minimizes the empirical risk, its estimation error is at most twice the uniform deviation \(\mathbb E_{S_n} \sup_{f\in\mathcal F} \big(R_\varphi(f) - R_\varphi^n(f)\big)\). Second, the *symmetrization* device introduces an independent ghost sample and random signs, bounding this uniform deviation by the Rademacher complexity of the loss class \(\{(x,y)\mapsto \varphi(y f(x)) : f \in \mathcal F\}\). Third, the *contraction principle* of Ledoux and Talagrand (1991) strips the \(L_\varphi\)-Lipschitz function \(\varphi\) at the price of the constant \(L_\varphi\), leaving the Rademacher complexity of \(\mathcal F\) itself. Collecting the constants yields the factor \(4 L_\varphi\). The symmetrization and concentration steps are by now standard machinery; Boucheron, Bousquet, and Lugosi (2005) survey them in a form tailored to classification.
:::

The concentration step deserves to be named, since it is the engine that turns a statement about averages into a statement holding for the worst function in the class at once. The tool is a bounded-differences inequality, which Shawe-Taylor and Cristianini (2004) take as the analytic backbone of their treatment.

::::: {.theorem #thm-11-7}
[Theorem (McDiarmid's bounded-differences inequality)]{.box-title}

Let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) be independent random variables taking values in a set \(\mathcal A\), and let \(F:\mathcal A^n \to \mathbb R\) change by a bounded amount whenever a single coordinate is altered:

$$\sup_{x_1,\dots,x_n,\,\hat x_i}\big| F(x_1,\dots,x_n) - F(x_1,\dots,\hat x_i,\dots,x_n)\big| \le c_i, \qquad 1 \le i \le n.$$

Then for every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\),

$$P\big\{ F(X_1,\dots,X_n) - \mathbb E\, F(X_1,\dots,X_n) \ge \varepsilon \big\} \le \exp\!\left(\frac{-2\varepsilon^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2} \right).$$

**Assumptions.** All domains, quantifiers, regularity conditions, and parameter restrictions stated in the result are in force; no converse is implied.
**Proof status.** No separate proof is attached to this result; independent technical review must determine whether the surrounding derivation is sufficient.
:::::

Hoeffding's inequality is the special case \(F(X_1,\dots,X_n) = \sum_i X_i\), and the deviation bound quoted for a single fixed function is nothing more than that case. The reason McDiarmid's form is the right one here is that the object we must control, the uniform deviation \(\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\big(R_\varphi(f) - R_\varphi^n(f)\big)\), is a function of the whole sample that no single example can move by much: swapping one training point shifts each empirical average, and hence their supremum, by at most a term of order \(1/n\). Feeding \(c_i\) of order \(1/n\) into the inequality shows this supremum concentrates around its expectation at the rate \(1/\sqrt n\), and that expectation is exactly what symmetrization rewrites as a Rademacher complexity. This is the source of the distribution-free \(1/\sqrt n\) tail on the estimation error.

The theorem quantifies the trade-off announced above:

- \(\mathcal F\) \"large\" means overfitting: the approximation error is small, but \(\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal F)\), and hence the estimation error, is large.
- \(\mathcal F\) \"small\" means underfitting: the estimation error is small, but the approximation error is large.

::::: {.remark}
[Remark (from expectation to high probability, and a data-dependent capacity)]{.box-title}

The bound above controls the excess risk on average over the draw of the training set. Shawe-Taylor and Cristianini (2004) give the companion statement that holds with high probability for *every* function in the class at once: fixing \(\delta \in (0,1)\), with probability at least \(1-\delta\) over the sample, every \(f \in \mathcal F\) satisfies

$$R_\varphi(f) \;\le\; R_\varphi^n(f) + \mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal F) + \sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2n}}.$$

Its proof is the recipe just described: bound the uniform deviation by its expectation with McDiarmid's inequality, then bound that expectation by the Rademacher complexity through symmetrization. The Rademacher term can itself be replaced by its *empirical* version

$$\widehat{\mathrm{Rad}}_n(\mathcal F) = \mathbb E_{\sigma}\!\left[ \sup_{f\in\mathcal F} \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i f(X_i) \;\Big|\; X_1,\dots,X_n \right],$$

which conditions on the observed inputs and averages over the signs alone, at the cost of enlarging the confidence term to \(3\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/2n}\). This empirical complexity is a quantity we can estimate directly from data by sampling sign patterns, so the capacity of the class becomes something we measure rather than postulate.
:::::

To use the bound we need classes \(\mathcal F\) that are flexible enough to keep the approximation error moderate, yet whose Rademacher complexity we can actually compute. Balls of an RKHS are exactly such classes.

## ERM in balls of an RKHS {#erm-in-rkhs-balls}

Assume now that \(\mathcal X\) is endowed with a positive definite kernel \(K\), with RKHS \(\mathcal H\) and feature map \(x \mapsto K_x = K(x,\cdot)\), so that the reproducing property \(f(x) = \langle f, K_x\rangle_{\mathcal H}\) holds for all \(f \in \mathcal H\). As function class for the ERM we take the ball of radius \(B\) in the RKHS:

$$\mathcal F_B = \big\{ f \in \mathcal H \;:\; \lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal H} \le B \big\}.$$

The radius \(B\) is our knob on capacity. The following theorem shows that the Rademacher complexity of \(\mathcal F_B\) is controlled by \(B\) and by the kernel alone, with no combinatorial argument anywhere: the reproducing property turns the supremum over the ball into a norm, and independence does the rest.

Before the proof, it is worth seeing why bounding a single number, the norm \(\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}\), should tame a whole infinite-dimensional class at all. The reproducing property answers this at every point at once. Writing \(f(x) = \langle f, K_x\rangle_{\mathcal H}\) and applying Cauchy-Schwarz,

$$\big|f(x)\big| = \big|\langle f, K_x\rangle_{\mathcal H}\big| \le \lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}\,\lVert K_x\rVert_{\mathcal H} = \lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}\,\sqrt{K(x,x)},$$

so a bound on the norm caps the height of every function in the ball, uniformly, by \(B\sqrt{K(x,x)}\). The same inequality applied to the difference \(f(x) - f(x') = \langle f, K_x - K_{x'}\rangle_{\mathcal H}\) controls how fast \(f\) can vary: two inputs whose feature vectors \(K_x\) and \(K_{x'}\) lie close together must receive nearby values, with the allowed gap scaled by \(\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}\). The RKHS norm is therefore a smoothness budget, and the radius \(B\) is how much of it we permit any function to spend. A large ball buys wiggly functions that can lunge at individual data points; a small ball admits only gentle, slowly varying ones, which is precisely the noise-resistant regime the Rademacher bound rewards. The theorem below turns this qualitative picture into a number.

:::: {.theorem #thm-11-8}
[Theorem (capacity control of RKHS balls)]{.box-title}

$$\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal F_B) \;\le\; \frac{2 B \sqrt{\mathbb E\, K(X,X)}}{\sqrt n}.$$

**Assumptions.** All domains, quantifiers, regularity conditions, and parameter restrictions stated in the result are in force; no converse is implied.
**Proof status.** Proved immediately below.
::::

::::::: {.proof}
[Proof]{.box-title}

Using the reproducing property and linearity of the inner product,

$$\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal F_B) = \mathbb E_{X,\sigma}\!\left[ \sup_{f\in\mathcal F_B} \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i f(X_i) \right] = \mathbb E_{X,\sigma}\!\left[ \sup_{f\in\mathcal F_B} \Big\langle f,\; \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i K_{X_i} \Big\rangle_{\mathcal H} \right].$$

For a fixed vector \(g \in \mathcal H\), the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives \(\langle f, g\rangle_{\mathcal H} \le B \lVert g\rVert_{\mathcal H}\) for all \(f \in \mathcal F_B\), with equality at \(f = B\, g / \lVert g\rVert_{\mathcal H}\) (and the supremum is trivially \(0 = B\lVert g\rVert_{\mathcal H}\) when \(g = 0\)). Hence

$$\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal F_B) = \mathbb E_{X,\sigma}\!\left[ B\, \Big\lVert \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i K_{X_i} \Big\rVert_{\mathcal H} \right] = \frac{2B}{n}\, \mathbb E_{X,\sigma}\!\left[ \sqrt{ \Big\lVert \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i K_{X_i} \Big\rVert_{\mathcal H}^2 } \right].$$

Expanding the squared norm with \(\langle K_{X_i}, K_{X_j}\rangle_{\mathcal H} = K(X_i, X_j)\), and applying Jensen's inequality to the concave square root,

$$\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal F_B) \le \frac{2B}{n} \sqrt{ \mathbb E_{X,\sigma}\!\left[ \sum_{i,j=1}^n \sigma_i \sigma_j K(X_i, X_j) \right] }.$$

But \(\mathbb E_\sigma[\sigma_i \sigma_j]\) is \(1\) if \(i = j\), \(0\) otherwise, and the \(\sigma_i\) are independent of the \(X_i\). Therefore

$$\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal F_B) \le \frac{2B}{n} \sqrt{ \mathbb E_X\!\left[ \sum_{i,j=1}^n \mathbb E_\sigma[\sigma_i\sigma_j]\, K(X_i,X_j) \right] } = \frac{2B}{n} \sqrt{ \mathbb E_X \sum_{i=1}^n K(X_i, X_i) } = \frac{2B\sqrt{\mathbb E_X K(X,X)}}{\sqrt n}. $$

[\(\square\)]{.qed}
:::::::

The decay in \(1/\sqrt n\) is the familiar parametric rate, yet \(\mathcal F_B\) may be a ball in an infinite-dimensional space. What replaces dimension is the average diagonal of the kernel, \(\mathbb E\, K(X,X)\), that is, the expected squared norm of the feature vector. In words, the capacity of the ball is fixed by just two quantities and nothing else: how large we let the functions grow, through the radius \(B\), and how large the feature vectors are, through \(\sqrt{\mathbb E\, K(X,X)}\), the whole divided by \(\sqrt n\) because averaging \(n\) independent sign terms shrinks their aligned part at exactly that rate. Dimension, the usual culprit behind overfitting, never appears. A function can inhabit an infinite-dimensional space and still be easy to learn, provided its norm is kept on a budget. Plugging the theorem into the learning bound gives the central statistical result of the chapter.

:::: {.corollary #cor-11-9}
[Corollary (basic learning bound in RKHS balls)]{.box-title}

Suppose \(K(X,X) \le \kappa^2\) almost surely (for instance the Gaussian kernel, with \(\kappa = 1\)). Then the ERM estimator in \(\mathcal F_B\) with an \(L_\varphi\)-Lipschitz loss satisfies

$$\mathbb E\, R_\varphi\big(\hat f_n\big) - R_\varphi^* \;\le\; \frac{8 L_\varphi \kappa B}{\sqrt n} \;+\; \left(\inf_{f\in\mathcal F_B} R_\varphi(f) - R_\varphi^* \right).$$

**Assumptions.** All domains, quantifiers, regularity conditions, and parameter restrictions stated in the result are in force; no converse is implied.
**Proof status.** No separate proof is attached to this result; independent technical review must determine whether the surrounding derivation is sufficient.
::::

::: {.remark}
[Remarks]{.box-title}

- \(B\) controls the trade-off between approximation and estimation error: enlarging the ball shrinks the approximation term and inflates the estimation term.
- The bound on the estimation error, \(8 L_\varphi \kappa B / \sqrt n\), is independent of the distribution \(P\) and decreases with \(n\): whatever the problem, the statistical part of the risk is under uniform control.
- The approximation error is harder to analyze in general; it depends on how well the ball can approach a minimizer of the \(\varphi\)-risk, hence on the interplay between the kernel and \(P\).
- In practice \(B\), or the regularization parameter \(\lambda\) introduced next, is tuned by cross-validation.
:::

::::: {.remark}
[Remark (the empirical version, and its combinatorial ancestor)]{.box-title}

The theorem bounds the Rademacher complexity in expectation over the inputs. Conditioning instead on the observed sample, the identical argument, with the sample now held fixed, yields the fully empirical bound of Shawe-Taylor and Cristianini (2004),

$$\widehat{\mathrm{Rad}}_n(\mathcal F_B) \;\le\; \frac{2B}{n}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n K(X_i,X_i)} \;=\; \frac{2B}{n}\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}\mathbf K},$$

where \(\mathbf K = \big(K(X_i,X_j)\big)_{i,j}\) is the kernel matrix. This is computable from the data alone: it replaces the population diagonal \(\mathbb E\, K(X,X)\) by the empirical trace of the Gram matrix, and it exposes the operational meaning of regularization. A learned function \(f = \sum_i \alpha_i K_{X_i}\) has norm \(\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H} = \sqrt{\alpha^\top \mathbf K \alpha}\), so shrinking \(\alpha^\top \mathbf K \alpha\) literally shrinks the radius \(B\) of the ball the solution occupies, and with it the capacity term.

It is instructive that the pre-Rademacher theory reached the same verdict by a wholly combinatorial route. Vapnik controlled capacity through the VC dimension, the largest number of points a class can label in all possible ways, and for large-margin hyperplanes he proved a bound that echoes ours exactly. In the account of Schölkopf and Smola (2002), the canonical hyperplanes \(x \mapsto \langle w, x\rangle\) with \(\lVert w\rVert \le \Lambda\), acting on data contained in a ball of radius \(R\) about the origin, have VC dimension

$$h \;\le\; R^2 \Lambda^2.$$

The message is the one the RKHS-ball bound delivers: capacity is governed not by the ambient dimension, which may be infinite, but by the product of a size of the data, \(R^2\) or \(\mathbb E\, K(X,X)\), and a budget on the norm, \(\Lambda^2\) or \(B^2\). Constraining \(\lVert w\rVert\), equivalently \(\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}\), is what makes an infinite-dimensional class learnable, and it is why the support vector machine introduced in [[ch:support-vector-machines]] minimizes the norm at all.
:::::

## Fast rates: local complexity and stability {#fast-rates-and-stability}

The corollary just proved is reassuring but pessimistic. It charges the estimation error at the rate \(B/\sqrt n\), the same \(1/\sqrt n\) that a single coin-flip average obeys and that the worst-case analyses of [[ch:vc-theory-and-generalization]] also reach, no matter how favourable the problem. Yet the empirical risk minimizer does not roam the whole ball \(\mathcal F_B\): it settles near the in-class risk minimizer, and functions near that optimum have small variance, so the sample pins them down far more reliably than it does a worst-case member of the ball. Two refinements exploit exactly this, and both can accelerate the rate from \(n^{-1/2}\) toward \(n^{-1}\). The first, *local Rademacher complexity*, measures capacity only inside a small-variance neighbourhood of the optimum and pays off under a variance condition on the loss. The second, *algorithmic stability*, abandons uniform convergence altogether and bounds the generalization gap of one specific estimator, which for the regularized kernel methods of [[ch:kernel-ridge-and-friends]] is immediate. This section develops both, following Bartlett, Bousquet, and Mendelson (2005) and Bousquet and Elisseeff (2002).

### Localization and the variance condition {#local-rademacher}

Return to the symmetrization heuristic behind the basic bound. The global complexity \(\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal F)\) prices the ability of the class to chase noise across all of \(\mathcal F\) at once. But the excess risk of the ERM is driven by the deviation of a single function, \(\hat f_n\), whose excess loss is small in mean and, if the loss is well behaved, small in variance too. Concentration is sharper for small-variance quantities: the Bernstein and Talagrand inequalities replace the range by the variance in their leading term, so a function of variance \(v\) deviates from its mean by \(O(\sqrt{v/n})\) rather than \(O(\sqrt{1/n})\). Restricting the complexity to a variance ball captures precisely this gain.

:::: {.definition #def-11-10}
[Definition (local Rademacher complexity)]{.box-title}

For a class \(\mathcal G\) of functions and a radius \(r \gt 0\), the *local Rademacher complexity* keeps only the members whose second moment is at most \(r\):

$$\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal G; r) = \mathbb E_{X,\sigma}\!\left[\, \sup_{g \in \mathcal G,\; \mathbb E g^2 \le r}\; \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i\, g(X_i) \right].$$

The global complexity is the case \(r = \infty\); shrinking \(r\) can only shrink the supremum, so \(\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal G; r) \le \mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal G)\).
::::

Localization helps only if a small excess risk genuinely forces a small variance, so that the excess loss of the ERM lives in a variance ball of small \(r\). That link is a hypothesis on the problem, not a theorem, and it is the exact condition that unlocks fast rates.

:::: {.definition #def-11-11}
[Definition (Bernstein variance condition)]{.box-title}

Let \(f^\star = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{f\in\mathcal F} R_\varphi(f)\) be the in-class risk minimizer and let \(g_f(x,y) = \varphi(y f(x)) - \varphi(y f^\star(x))\) be the excess loss of \(f\). The class satisfies the *\((B,\theta)\)-Bernstein condition*, for constants \(B \gt 0\) and \(\theta \in (0,1]\), if

$$\mathbb E\big[g_f^2\big] \;\le\; B\,\big(\mathbb E\, g_f\big)^{\theta} \;=\; B\,\big(R_\varphi(f) - R_\varphi(f^\star)\big)^{\theta} \qquad \text{for every } f \in \mathcal F.$$

The strongest case \(\theta = 1\) reads: the variance of the excess loss is at most \(B\) times the excess risk itself.
::::

Read plainly, whenever a function is close to optimal in mean (small \(\mathbb E g_f\)) it is automatically close in mean square (small \(\mathbb E g_f^2\)), so the near-minimizers cluster in a thin variance shell that localization can exploit. The condition is not exotic. For the squared loss on a bounded convex class it holds with \(\theta = 1\), because the excess risk equals \(\lVert f - f^\star\rVert_{L^2}^2\), which also controls the variance of the excess loss. In binary classification it follows from the low-noise (margin) conditions on \(\eta\) taken up in [[ch:vc-theory-and-generalization]], and the calibration theory of the previous sections, due to Bartlett, Jordan, and McAuliffe (2006), converts such a margin condition into exactly this Bernstein bound on the excess \(\varphi\)-risk. When \(\theta \to 0\) the condition is vacuous and no acceleration is possible; \(\theta = 1\) buys the fastest rate.

### The sub-root fixed point that sets the rate {#the-fixed-point}

With the variance condition in hand the rate is decided by a single scalar: the radius at which the local complexity stops shrinking faster than the radius itself. To state it we need the shape that local Rademacher complexities always take as a function of \(r\).

:::: {.definition #def-11-12}
[Definition (sub-root function, critical radius)]{.box-title}

A function \(\psi : [0,\infty) \to [0,\infty)\) is *sub-root* if it is nondecreasing, not identically zero, and \(r \mapsto \psi(r)/\sqrt r\) is nonincreasing. A sub-root function has a unique positive fixed point \(r^\star\), the *critical radius*, solving

$$\psi(r^\star) = r^\star.$$
::::

The map \(r \mapsto \mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal G; r)\), applied to the star-hull of the class so that the ratio condition holds, is sub-root: the supremum grows with \(r\), but its \(\sqrt r\)-normalized version falls, because widening the variance ball admits functions of larger norm only at a diminishing marginal rate. The theorem of the localized theory says the excess risk is set by where this sub-root bound meets the diagonal.

:::: {.theorem #thm-11-13}
[Theorem (fast rate from localization; Bartlett, Bousquet, and Mendelson, 2005)]{.box-title}

Suppose the excess-loss class satisfies the \((B,1)\)-Bernstein condition and that \(\psi\) is a sub-root upper bound on its local Rademacher complexity, \(\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal G; r) \le \psi(r)\), with critical radius \(r^\star\). Then there are constants \(c_1, c_2\) depending only on \(B\) and the range of the loss such that, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), the empirical risk minimizer obeys

$$R_\varphi(\hat f_n) - \inf_{f\in\mathcal F} R_\varphi(f) \;\le\; c_1\, r^\star \;+\; \frac{c_2\,\ln(1/\delta)}{n}.$$

**Assumptions.** All domains, quantifiers, regularity conditions, and parameter restrictions stated in the result are in force; no converse is implied.
**Proof status.** No separate proof is attached to this result; independent technical review must determine whether the surrounding derivation is sufficient.
::::

The estimation error is therefore \(O(r^\star)\), the critical radius, plus a benign \(1/n\) term. Everything now rests on how the critical radius behaves, and for a kernel class it is read straight off the spectrum. The localized companion of the trace bound proved above, also due to Bartlett, Bousquet, and Mendelson (2005), controls the local complexity of the unit RKHS ball by the truncated eigenvalue sum of the kernel integral operator,

$$\mathrm{Rad}_n(\mathcal F_1; r) \;\le\; \sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^\infty \min(\lambda_i,\, r)},$$

where \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots\) are the Mercer eigenvalues of [[ch:mercer-and-rates]]. The right-hand side is sub-root in \(r\), so the rate is fixed by the fixed point of

$$r \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^\infty \min(\lambda_i,\, r)},$$

where the constant has been dropped, since it only rescales \(r^\star\). The truncation \(\min(\lambda_i, r)\) is the whole mechanism: an eigen-direction with \(\lambda_i \gt r\) is \"large\" and contributes its full \(r\) to the budget, while the tail directions with \(\lambda_i \lt r\) contribute only their own small energy. The count of large directions, \(d(r) = \#\{i : \lambda_i \gt r\}\), is the *effective dimension* at scale \(r\), and the fixed point balances this growing dimension against the shrinking tail. When the spectrum decays polynomially that balance has a closed-form exponent, and it is genuinely faster than \(n^{-1/2}\).

[Example (critical radius for polynomial eigen-decay)]{.box-title}

Solve the fixed point \(r = \sqrt{\tfrac1n \sum_i \min(\lambda_i, r)}\) for \(n = 100, 1000, 10000\), and compare its decay with the slow rate \(n^{-1/2}\) and the predicted fast rate \(n^{-2b/(2b+1)} = n^{-2/3}\). The values are independently reproducible from the chapter's computational reference [@kernelbook-code-ch04-ex1].

2.  [Solve at three sample sizes.]{.wex-op} The fixed point \(r^\star\) and the effective dimension \(d(r^\star) = \#\{i : \lambda_i \gt r^\star\}\) are

$$\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}

**Reading.** The critical radius, and with it the excess risk, decays like \(n^{-2/3}\) for this kernel, not \(n^{-1/2}\): localization has turned the slow global bound into a genuinely fast rate. The engine is the effective dimension \(d(r^\star)\), which grows only like \(n^{1/3}\) (here \(3, 8, 17\)), so the sample faces an effectively low-dimensional problem even though the RKHS is infinite-dimensional. The smoother the kernel (the larger \(b\)), the fewer the large eigen-directions and the faster the rate.

[Remark (the rate, in one line)]{.box-title}

For \(\lambda_i \asymp i^{-2b}\) with \(b \gt \tfrac12\), splitting the sum at the crossover index \(i \asymp r^{-1/(2b)}\) gives \(\sum_i \min(\lambda_i, r) \asymp r^{\,1 - 1/(2b)}\), so the fixed-point equation \(r^2 \asymp n^{-1}\, r^{\,1-1/(2b)}\) collapses to

$$r^\star \asymp n^{-\frac{2b}{2b+1}}.$$

As \(b \to \tfrac12^+\) this degrades to the slow \(n^{-1/2}\); as \(b \to \infty\), the smoother the kernel, the closer the rate approaches the parametric ceiling \(n^{-1}\). ### Generalization from algorithmic stability {#stability-route}

Localization still argues through uniform convergence over a class. A completely different route asks nothing about classes at all: it looks only at how much the *output* of a fixed learning algorithm moves when one training point is perturbed. If the algorithm is insensitive to any single example, then its empirical risk cannot sit far from its true risk, because no example was able to bend the fit toward itself. This is the stability programme of Bousquet and Elisseeff (2002), and it is tailor-made for the regularized kernel estimators of [[ch:kernel-ridge-and-friends]].

:::: {.definition #def-11-14}
[Definition (uniform stability)]{.box-title}

Let \(A\) map a training sample \(S = (z_1,\dots,z_n)\) to a predictor \(A_S\), and let \(S^{\setminus i}\) be \(S\) with its \(i\)-th example removed. The algorithm has *uniform stability* \(\beta_n\) with respect to a loss \(\ell\) if

$$\sup_{z}\;\big|\ell(A_S, z) - \ell(A_{S^{\setminus i}}, z)\big| \;\le\; \beta_n \qquad \text{for all samples } S \text{ and all } i.$$
::::

::::: {.theorem #thm-11-15}
[Theorem (stability implies generalization; Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002)]{.box-title}

If \(A\) has uniform stability \(\beta_n\) and the loss is bounded, \(0 \le \ell \le M\), then the generalization gap is controlled in expectation,

$$\Big|\, \mathbb E_S\big[R(A_S) - R^n(A_S)\big]\Big| \;\le\; 2\beta_n,$$

and, with probability at least \(1-\delta\) over the sample,

$$R(A_S) \;\le\; R^n(A_S) + 2\beta_n + \big(4 n\,\beta_n + M\big)\sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2n}}.$$

**Assumptions.** All domains, quantifiers, regularity conditions, and parameter restrictions stated in the result are in force; no converse is implied.
**Proof status.** No separate proof is attached to this result; independent technical review must determine whether the surrounding derivation is sufficient.
:::::

For the bound to say anything, the stability must vanish faster than \(1/\sqrt n\): a stability of order \(1/n\) makes the expected gap \(O(1/n)\), a fast rate, and keeps the high-probability term at \(O(1/\sqrt n)\). The decisive fact is that regularization in an RKHS delivers exactly \(\beta_n = O(1/n)\), and the reason is the strong convexity that the norm penalty injects into the objective.

::::: {.proposition #prop-11-16}
[Proposition (stability of regularized kernel methods; Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002)]{.box-title}

Let \(\ell(f,z)\) be convex and \(L\)-Lipschitz in the prediction \(f(x)\), and let \(K(x,x) \le \kappa^2\). The regularized estimator

$$\hat f_S = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{f\in\mathcal H}\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(f, z_i) + \lambda\, \lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}^2$$

has uniform stability

$$\beta_n \;\le\; \frac{L^2 \kappa^2}{2\lambda n}.$$

In particular the support vector machine (hinge loss, \(L = 1\)) and kernel ridge regression (squared loss on a bounded range) are uniformly stable at the rate \(1/n\) for fixed \(\lambda\).

**Assumptions.** All domains, quantifiers, regularity conditions, and parameter restrictions stated in the result are in force; no converse is implied.
**Proof status.** Proved immediately below.
:::::

:::::: {.proof}
[Proof]{.box-title}

Write \(R_\lambda(f) = \tfrac1n\sum_i \ell(f,z_i) + \lambda\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}^2\) for the objective on \(S\) and \(R_\lambda^{\setminus k}\) for the objective on \(S^{\setminus k}\) (both empirical averages normalized by \(n\)), with minimizers \(\hat f = \hat f_S\) and \(\hat g = \hat f_{S^{\setminus k}}\). The penalty makes each objective \(2\lambda\)-strongly convex in \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal H}\), so at the minimizer \(\hat f\),

$$R_\lambda(\hat g) - R_\lambda(\hat f) \;\ge\; \lambda\,\lVert \hat g - \hat f\rVert_{\mathcal H}^2,$$

and symmetrically \(R_\lambda^{\setminus k}(\hat f) - R_\lambda^{\setminus k}(\hat g) \ge \lambda\lVert \hat f - \hat g\rVert_{\mathcal H}^2\). Adding the two inequalities, the regularization terms cancel and the empirical averages differ only in the single example \(z_k\), leaving

$$2\lambda\,\lVert \hat f - \hat g\rVert_{\mathcal H}^2 \;\le\; \frac1n\big|\ell(\hat f, z_k) - \ell(\hat g, z_k)\big| \;\le\; \frac{L}{n}\,\big|\hat f(x_k) - \hat g(x_k)\big|.$$

The reproducing property and Cauchy-Schwarz bound the prediction gap by the norm gap, \(|\hat f(x_k) - \hat g(x_k)| = |\langle \hat f - \hat g,\, K_{x_k}\rangle_{\mathcal H}| \le \kappa\,\lVert \hat f - \hat g\rVert_{\mathcal H}\). Substituting and dividing by \(\lVert \hat f - \hat g\rVert_{\mathcal H}\) gives

$$\lVert \hat f - \hat g\rVert_{\mathcal H} \;\le\; \frac{L\kappa}{2\lambda n}.$$

Finally the loss is \(L\)-Lipschitz in the prediction and the prediction is \(\kappa\)-Lipschitz in the norm, so for any test point \(z\), \(|\ell(\hat f, z) - \ell(\hat g, z)| \le L\kappa\,\lVert \hat f - \hat g\rVert_{\mathcal H} \le L^2\kappa^2/(2\lambda n)\), which is the claimed \(\beta_n\). [\(\square\)]{.qed}
::::::

Two conclusions close the loop with the localized theory. The expected generalization gap of the support vector machine and of kernel ridge regression is at most \(2\beta_n \le L^2\kappa^2/(\lambda n)\), a clean \(1/n\) bound obtained without a single covering number or Rademacher average, purely from the geometry of the regularized objective. And the appearance of \(\lambda\) in the denominator is the same trade-off seen throughout the chapter, now read through stability: a larger penalty shrinks the ball, stiffens the solution against perturbation, and improves the gap, at the price of the approximation error a smaller ball incurs. Balancing \(\beta_n\) against that approximation error, exactly as the radius \(B\) was tuned in the corollary, recovers the fast rates of the localized analysis from the algorithmic side, which is why Steinwart and Christmann (2008) treat stability and local complexity as two windows onto the same oracle inequality.

## From constrained ERM to penalized ERM: regularization {#from-constraints-to-penalties}

The theory above analyzes ERM over the ball \(\mathcal F_B\), which is the constrained minimization problem

$$\begin{cases} \displaystyle \min_{f\in\mathcal H}\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \varphi\big(y_i f(x_i)\big) \\[2pt] \text{subject to}\;\; \lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H} \le B. \end{cases}$$

The algorithms of [[ch:kernel-ridge-and-friends]] solve something else: an unconstrained problem with a norm penalty. The bridge between the two is convex duality. To make the problem practical we assume that \(\varphi\) is convex. The objective is then convex in \(f\), since each map \(f \mapsto \varphi(y_i \langle f, K_{x_i}\rangle_{\mathcal H})\) composes the convex \(\varphi\) with a continuous linear functional of \(f\), and the constraint set is a convex ball. The problem is thus a convex problem in \(f\) for which strong duality holds (the interlude below develops the machinery; Slater's condition is trivially satisfied here since the constraint can be made strict). In particular, \(f\) solves the constrained problem if and only if it solves, for some dual parameter \(\lambda \ge 0\), the unconstrained problem

$$\min_{f\in\mathcal H}\; \left\{ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \varphi\big(y_i f(x_i)\big) + \lambda\, \lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}^2 \right\}.$$

The penalized formulation is the one used in practice: it is unconstrained, the representer theorem reduces it to a finite-dimensional convex program in the coefficients of \(f = \sum_i \alpha_i K_{x_i}\), and the parameter \(\lambda\) plays the role of \(1/B\): larger \(\lambda\) means a smaller effective ball. The learning bounds for \(\mathcal F_B\) therefore transfer to the penalized estimators our algorithms compute, and they explain *why* regularization works: the penalty is not a numerical convenience but a capacity constraint, and the corollary above prices exactly how much statistical safety each unit of \(\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}\) costs.

This completes the design of large-margin classifiers, which is worth recording as a summary of the whole program:

$$\min_{f\in\mathcal H}\; \left\{ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \varphi\big(y_i f(x_i)\big) + \lambda \lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}^2 \right\}$$

- \(\varphi\) calibrated (for example decreasing with \(\varphi'(0) \lt 0\)) implies the \(\varphi\)-risk is a good proxy for the classification error, by the calibration theorem;
- \(\varphi\) convex, combined with the representer theorem, implies efficient algorithms.

## Interlude: convex optimization and Lagrangian duality {#convex-duality-interlude}

The equivalence between constrained and penalized ERM invoked strong duality, and the derivation of the support vector machine in [[ch:support-vector-machines]] leaned on it even harder. This interlude develops the needed facts from scratch, for a general constrained problem; no convexity is assumed until it is needed. The treatment follows the standard development in Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004).

### Primal, dual, and the duality gap {#duality-gap}

The picture to keep in mind is two curves: the primal objective \(f(x)\) is a curve whose minimum \(f(x^\star)\) we want; duality constructs a second function \(q(\nu)\), the dual, every value of which sits below every value of \(f\) on the feasible set. *Strong duality* means the two meet:

$$\max_\nu q(\nu) = \min_x f(x).$$

Strong duality holds in most \"reasonable cases\" of convex optimization, in a sense made precise below. Two caveats accompany the picture. First, the relation between the primal optimum \(x^\star\) and the dual optimum \(\nu^\star\) is not always known a priori. Second, and more usefully, even without solving anything to optimality, any candidate pair \((\tilde x, \tilde\nu)\) certifies its own quality through the *duality gap* \(\delta(\tilde x,\tilde\nu) = f(\tilde x) - q(\tilde\nu)\), since

$$0 \le f(\tilde x) - f(x^\star) \le \delta(\tilde x, \tilde \nu).$$

This makes duality a practical stopping criterion, not only a theoretical device. Dual problems are typically obtained by Lagrangian or Fenchel duality; we develop the Lagrangian route.

### The Lagrangian and the dual function {#lagrangian-duality}

The dual function promised above is built from the constraints themselves, so we start from the most general problem we will need. Consider an equality and inequality constrained optimization problem over a variable \(x \in \mathcal X\):

$$\begin{aligned} \text{minimize} \quad & f(x) \\ \text{subject to} \quad & h_i(x) = 0, \quad i = 1,\dots,m, \\ & g_j(x) \le 0, \quad j = 1,\dots,r, \end{aligned}$$

making no assumption on \(f\), \(g\) and \(h\). Denote by \(f^\star\) the optimal value of the decision function under the constraints, that is, \(f^\star = f(x^\star)\) if the minimum is reached at a global minimum \(x^\star\).

::::: {.definition #def-11-17}
[Definition (Lagrangian, dual function)]{.box-title}

The *Lagrangian* of this problem is the function \(L : \mathcal X \times \mathbb R^m \times \mathbb R^r \to \mathbb R\) defined by

$$L(x, \lambda, \mu) = f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i h_i(x) + \sum_{j=1}^r \mu_j g_j(x).$$

The *Lagrange dual function* \(q : \mathbb R^m \times \mathbb R^r \to \mathbb R \cup \{-\infty\}\) is

$$q(\lambda,\mu) = \inf_{x\in\mathcal X} L(x,\lambda,\mu) = \inf_{x\in\mathcal X}\left(f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i h_i(x) + \sum_{j=1}^r \mu_j g_j(x) \right).$$
:::::

The multipliers \(\lambda_i\) and \(\mu_j\) price the constraints: the Lagrangian relaxes the hard constraints into linear charges on their violation. For the primal problem above, the *Lagrange dual problem* is

$$\begin{aligned} \text{maximize} \quad & q(\lambda, \mu) \\ \text{subject to} \quad & \mu \ge 0. \end{aligned}$$

::: {.proposition #prop-11-18}
[Proposition]{.box-title}

- \(q\) is concave in \((\lambda,\mu)\), even if the original problem is not convex.
- The dual function yields lower bounds on the optimal value \(f^\star\) of the original problem when \(\mu\) is non-negative:

$$q(\lambda,\mu) \le f^\star, \qquad \forall \lambda \in \mathbb R^m,\; \forall \mu \in \mathbb R^r,\; \mu \ge 0.$$

**Assumptions.** All domains, quantifiers, regularity conditions, and parameter restrictions stated in the result are in force; no converse is implied.
**Proof status.** Proved immediately below.
:::

::::::: {.proof}
[Proof]{.box-title}

Recall that

$$L(x,\lambda,\mu) = f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i h_i(x) + \sum_{j=1}^r \mu_j g_j(x).$$

For each fixed \(x\), the function \((\lambda,\mu) \mapsto L(x,\lambda,\mu)\) is affine, and therefore both convex and concave in \((\lambda,\mu)\). The pointwise infimum of a family of concave functions is concave, therefore \(q\) is concave.

Let \(\bar x\) be any feasible point, that is, \(h(\bar x) = 0\) and \(g(\bar x) \le 0\). Then we have, for any \(\lambda\) and any \(\mu \ge 0\),

$$\sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i h_i(\bar x) + \sum_{j=1}^r \mu_j g_j(\bar x) \le 0,$$

hence

$$L(\bar x, \lambda, \mu) = f(\bar x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i h_i(\bar x) + \sum_{j=1}^r \mu_j g_j(\bar x) \le f(\bar x),$$

and therefore

$$q(\lambda,\mu) = \inf_{x} L(x,\lambda,\mu) \le L(\bar x,\lambda,\mu) \le f(\bar x)$$

for every feasible \(\bar x\). Taking the infimum over feasible \(\bar x\) gives \(q(\lambda,\mu) \le f^\star\). [\(\square\)]{.qed}
:::::::

### Weak and strong duality {#weak-and-strong-duality}

Let \(q^\star\) be the optimal value of the Lagrange dual problem. Each \(q(\lambda,\mu)\) with \(\mu \ge 0\) is a lower bound for \(f^\star\), and by definition \(q^\star\) is the best lower bound so obtained. The following *weak duality* inequality therefore always holds:

$$q^\star \le f^\star.$$

This inequality holds even when \(q^\star\) or \(f^\star\) is infinite. The difference \(f^\star - q^\star \ge 0\) is called the *optimal duality gap* of the original problem.

We say that *strong duality* holds if the optimal duality gap is zero, that is,

$$q^\star = f^\star.$$

If strong duality holds, then the best lower bound obtainable from the Lagrange dual function is tight. Strong duality does not hold for general nonlinear problems, but it usually holds for convex problems; conditions that ensure it for convex problems are called *constraint qualifications*. When it holds, we have for all feasible primal and dual points \(x\), \((\lambda,\mu)\),

$$q(\lambda,\mu) \le q(\lambda^\star,\mu^\star) = L(x^\star,\lambda^\star,\mu^\star) = f(x^\star) \le f(x),$$

so any feasible dual point brackets the optimum from below and any feasible primal point brackets it from above.

::::: {.theorem #thm-11-19}
[Theorem (Slater's constraint qualification)]{.box-title}

Strong duality holds for a convex problem

$$\begin{aligned} \text{minimize} \quad & f(x) \\ \text{subject to} \quad & g_j(x) \le 0, \quad j = 1,\dots,r, \\ & Ax = b, \end{aligned}$$

if it is *strictly feasible*, that is, if there exists at least one feasible point satisfying

$$g_j(x) \lt 0, \quad j = 1,\dots,r, \qquad Ax = b.$$

**Assumptions.** All domains, quantifiers, regularity conditions, and parameter restrictions stated in the result are in force; no converse is implied.
**Proof status.** No separate proof is attached to this result; independent technical review must determine whether the surrounding derivation is sufficient.
:::::

::: {.remark}
[Remarks]{.box-title}

- Slater's condition also ensures that the maximum \(q^\star\), when \(q^\star \gt -\infty\), is *attained*: there exists a point \((\lambda^\star,\mu^\star)\) with \(q(\lambda^\star,\mu^\star) = q^\star = f^\star\).
- The condition can be sharpened: for example, strict feasibility is not required for affine constraints.
- Many other types of constraint qualifications exist.
:::

### Dual optimal pairs and complementary slackness {#complementary-slackness}

Suppose that strong duality holds, \(x^\star\) is primal optimal, and \((\lambda^\star, \mu^\star)\) is dual optimal. Then we have

$$\begin{aligned} f(x^\star) = q(\lambda^\star, \mu^\star) &= \inf_{x} \left(f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i^\star h_i(x) + \sum_{j=1}^r \mu_j^\star g_j(x) \right) \\ &\le f(x^\star) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i^\star h_i(x^\star) + \sum_{j=1}^r \mu_j^\star g_j(x^\star) \\ &\le f(x^\star), \end{aligned}$$

where the last step uses \(h_i(x^\star) = 0\), \(\mu_j^\star \ge 0\) and \(g_j(x^\star) \le 0\). Hence both inequalities are in fact equalities, and each carries information.

The first equality, read at the point \(x^\star\), shows that

$$L(x^\star, \lambda^\star, \mu^\star) = \inf_{x} L(x, \lambda^\star, \mu^\star),$$

that is, \(x^\star\) minimizes the Lagrangian at \((\lambda^\star,\mu^\star)\): once the optimal multipliers are known, the constrained primal problem collapses to an unconstrained minimization. This is precisely the mechanism that converted ERM over an RKHS ball into penalized ERM, with \(\lambda\) the optimal multiplier of the norm constraint. The second equality gives the following important property.

:::: {.proposition #prop-11-20}
[Proposition (complementary slackness)]{.box-title}

Each optimal Lagrange multiplier is zero unless the corresponding constraint is active at the optimum:

$$\mu_j^\star\, g_j(x^\star) = 0, \qquad j = 1,\dots,r.$$

**Assumptions.** All domains, quantifiers, regularity conditions, and parameter restrictions stated in the result are in force; no converse is implied.
**Proof status.** Proved immediately below.
::::

::: {.proof}
[Proof]{.box-title}

The second equality in the chain above states that \(\sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i^\star h_i(x^\star) + \sum_{j=1}^r \mu_j^\star g_j(x^\star) = 0\), and the equality-constraint terms vanish since \(h_i(x^\star) = 0\), leaving \(\sum_{j=1}^r \mu_j^\star g_j(x^\star) = 0\). Each summand satisfies \(\mu_j^\star g_j(x^\star) \le 0\), being the product of a non-negative and a non-positive number; a sum of non-positive terms equals zero only if every term is zero. [\(\square\)]{.qed}
:::

Complementary slackness says that inactive constraints, those satisfied with room to spare at the optimum, cost nothing: their prices \(\mu_j^\star\) vanish. Only constraints that actually bind can carry positive multipliers. In kernel methods this is the source of *sparsity*: solutions supported on the few data points whose constraints are active.

## The support vector machine in this light {#towards-support-vector-machines}

This machinery is exactly what underlies the support vector machine (SVM) introduced in [[ch:support-vector-machines]]. That classifier is the penalized ERM problem of this chapter with one particular choice of calibrated convex loss function, the hinge loss \(\varphi(u) = \max(1-u,\, 0)\). Historically it was the first \"kernel method\" for pattern recognition, and it remains the most popular, often state-of-the-art in performance. Two features distinguish it, and both are consequences of the duality theory developed here. The hinge loss leads to a *sparse* solution: by complementary slackness, not all points are involved in the decomposition \(f = \sum_i \alpha_i K_{x_i}\), a form of compression that keeps only the support vectors. And the structure of the dual problem enables a particular algorithm for fast optimization, decomposition by chunking methods, which scales the approach to large training sets. Seen from the vantage of this chapter, the calibration, capacity, and duality results are precisely what those properties of [[ch:support-vector-machines]] rest on.

::: {.remark}
[A modern caveat: interpolation]{.box-title}

The bounds of this chapter share a classical premise: capacity must be controlled, so a predictor that fits the training data exactly should generalize badly. Modern practice complicates that picture. Belkin, Hsu, Ma, and Mandal (2019) showed that test error, plotted against model size, can descend, rise to a peak at the point where the model just interpolates the data, and then descend a second time, the *double descent* curve; and kernel \"ridgeless\" regression, exact interpolation with no penalty, can generalize when the kernel's spectrum decays at the right rate (Liang and Rakhlin, 2020). The RKHS-norm capacity here is not wrong, but it is only part of the story: *which* interpolant a method selects, and how the eigenvalue tail absorbs noise, matters as much as whether it interpolates. This is taken up in [[ch:kernels-now|Kernels in the Modern World]].
:::

::: {.remark}
[Further reading]{.box-title}

The statistical learning theory behind these bounds is developed in Schölkopf and Smola (2002), *Learning with Kernels*, chapter 5, and in Shawe-Taylor and Cristianini (2004), *Kernel Methods for Pattern Analysis*, chapter 4. For the Rademacher-complexity machinery and its use for kernel classes see Bartlett and Mendelson (2002), and for the surrogate-loss calibration theory Bartlett, Jordan, and McAuliffe (2006). A comprehensive account is Steinwart and Christmann (2008), *Support Vector Machines*.
:::

## Common mistakes and practical implications {#common-mistakes-and-practical-implications}

- Do not confuse empirical fit with estimation error. The latter is a uniform, probabilistic statement about a data-selected predictor.
- Keep the norm radius \(B\), diagonal bound \(\kappa^2\), loss Lipschitz constant, confidence level, and sample size visible when quoting a Rademacher bound.
- Calibration says that minimizing the population surrogate risk recovers the Bayes sign; it does not say a finite sample, finite ball, or imperfect optimizer reaches that minimum.
- A fast rate needs more than boundedness. Identify the curvature, noise, localization, or stability condition that improves the global \(n^{-1/2}\) scale.
- Select kernel and regularization using training and validation data only, then report the non-kernel baseline and the resulting RKHS norm.

## Summary and further reading {#summary-and-further-reading}

Learning in an RKHS ball balances approximation against estimation: a larger ball can represent more targets but can also correlate more strongly with sample noise. Rademacher complexity converts the geometry of the ball into a finite-sample generalization penalty through the reproducing property, while calibration connects optimized surrogate risk to the classification decision we ultimately care about. Local complexity and stability explain when global bounds are conservative, and Lagrange duality connects an explicit norm budget to regularized empirical risk. For primary treatments, see [@bartlett2002], [@bartlett2006], [@vapnik1995], and [@steinwart2008].
