Kernels: The Geometry of Learning

A book-form synthesis of the kernel-methods literature

This book synthesizes three standard sources: the lecture course "Machine Learning with Kernel Methods" by Julien Mairal and Jean-Philippe Vert (kernel-learning.github.io), and the textbooks "Learning with Kernels" by Bernhard Schölkopf and Alexander Smola (2002) and "Kernel Methods for Pattern Analysis" by John Shawe-Taylor and Nello Cristianini (2004). All results and their attributions follow these sources; the connecting prose, the pedagogical arc, and the interactive figures are this book's.

    0 · Preliminaries

    A self-contained reference for the mathematics the book leans on, drawn from linear algebra, analysis and Hilbert spaces, probability, optimization, and functional analysis. Read it front to back for a refresher, or dip in when a later chapter cites a result by name.

  1. ·Mathematical Preliminaries
    2. I · The Idea

    Kernel methods rest on one move: compare examples with a similarity function, and a surprising amount of learning follows. This part builds the idea from the ground up, from the definition of a valid similarity to the space it secretly defines and the theorem that makes computation possible.

    Fix a kernel and a supervised task. This part derives the two workhorse algorithms, kernel ridge regression and the support vector machine, as one recipe under two loss functions, then bounds what they can learn with the statistical theory of RKHS balls.

    The same Gram matrix that drives supervised learning also drives the unsupervised kind. Kernel PCA, clustering, and canonical correlation analysis are the classical structure-finding algorithms, each rewritten to see only the kernel.

    Why did any of it work? This part opens the RKHS with Mercer's theorem, reads its spectrum as a measure of smoothness, and turns eigenvalue decay into concrete rates of convergence.

    Everything so far assumed a kernel was handed to you. This part builds them: for vectors by symmetry and probability, and for the structured data, sequences and graphs, where the kernel is the only bridge to the RKHS toolbox.

    13. VI · Kernels and Distributions

    A kernel can embed not just a point but an entire probability distribution. This part builds the mean embedding, measures distributions by the distance between embeddings, and asks which kernels lose no information in the process.

  13. 12Mean Embeddings, MMD, and Characteristic Kernels
    14. VII · Scaling and the Modern View

    The exact solve does not survive a million examples, and the kernel need not be fixed or shallow. This part removes the quadratic wall, learns the kernel itself, and closes by reading deep networks as kernel machines.

  14. 13Large-Scale Kernel Machines

    17. VIII · Kernels Now

    The two standard textbooks close around 2004, before the deep-learning era rewrote what kernels are for. This part is a guide to what the literature produced after them: the interpolation revolution that revised the book's own theory of generalization, the infinite-width limits that made deep networks kernel machines, kernels that finally scale to millions of points, distributions and attention read through the RKHS, and the frontier where kernels give way to feature learning.
