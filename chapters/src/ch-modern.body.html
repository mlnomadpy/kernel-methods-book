Kernels in the Modern World

The two textbooks this book synthesizes were finished around 2004, and the lecture course a decade later; between them and now, deep learning arrived and changed the questions kernels are asked to answer. This chapter is a map of the modern literature: what the field produced after the classical account, why it matters, and where each piece connects back to the chapters you have read. It is a survey rather than a derivation, meant to point you toward the primary sources with enough context to know why each one is worth reading.

The interpolation revolution

The most consequential modern result is that a central lesson of Parts II and IV is incomplete. The classical theory says a model that fits training data exactly, an interpolant, must generalize badly, because it has spent all its capacity memorizing noise; the RKHS-ball bounds of Chapter 6 and the bias-variance decomposition of Chapter 7 both rest on that intuition. Modern practice contradicts it, and kernels turned out to be the cleanest setting in which to understand why.

Double descent

Belkin, Hsu, Ma, and Mandal (2019) showed that test error, plotted against model size, does not follow the classical U. It descends, then rises to a peak exactly at the interpolation threshold where the model has just enough parameters to fit the data exactly, and then descends a second time as the model grows further into the overparameterized regime. Random-feature ridge regression is the canonical model of the effect: as the number of features \(D\) crosses the sample size \(n\), the feature matrix becomes square and nearly singular, so the interpolating solution has enormous norm and generalizes terribly; past \(D = n\) there are many interpolating solutions and the minimum-norm one is smooth again.

This reframes overfitting as a question about which interpolant, not whether to interpolate. Belkin, Ma, and Mandal (2018) argued the point in the paper's title, that to understand deep learning we need to understand kernel learning, since kernel machines also interpolate noisy data and still generalize. The theory that followed is largely a story about the kernel's spectrum. Liang and Rakhlin (2020) showed that kernel "ridgeless" regression, exact interpolation with no regularization, can generalize when the eigenvalues decay at the right rate, and Bartlett, Long, Lugosi, and Tsigler (2020) gave the matching characterization of benign overfitting in linear regression: harmless interpolation happens exactly when the spectrum has a long tail of small eigenvalues that absorb the noise without distorting the signal. The eigenvalue decay that Chapter 7 read as a smoothness scale is, in this modern light, also the dial that decides whether interpolation is safe.

Infinite width: the deep-learning bridge, completed

Chapter 14 introduced the neural tangent kernel as the object governing a very wide network's training. Two neighboring limits complete the picture and turn "networks are related to kernels" into a precise correspondence.

The first is the neural network Gaussian process (NNGP). Neal's (1996) observation that an infinitely wide untrained network is a Gaussian process was extended by Lee et al. (2018) and Matthews et al. (2018) to deep networks: at random initialization, a wide net of any depth is exactly a Gaussian process whose covariance, the NNGP kernel, is computed layer by layer. Where the NNGP describes the network before training, the neural tangent kernel of Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler (2018) describes it during training, and Arora et al. (2019) made the convolutional version (the CNTK) exactly computable and competitive on small data. Yang's (2019) Tensor Programs framework then showed both kernels can be derived for essentially any architecture, so the infinite-width kernel of a transformer or a residual network is as well-defined as that of a two-layer net.

The two infinite-width limits

There is a second limit, and it is the one that explains the boundary of the kernel view. In the lazy or NTK regime the features do not move, so the network is exactly a kernel machine and does no representation learning. In the mean-field regime of Chizat and Bach (2018) and Mei, Montanari, and Nguyen (2018), the width is taken to infinity with a different scaling, and now the hidden units do move, following a gradient flow on the space of probability distributions over neurons. Feature learning lives in the mean-field limit and is absent from the kernel limit, which is the precise sense in which a network can outperform its own tangent kernel, and the theoretical companion to the failure of the fixed kernel neuron discussed at the end of Chapter 14.

The function space itself tells the same story. The set of functions a single infinitely-wide hidden layer can represent with bounded weights is not an RKHS but a strictly larger space, the variation-norm or Barron space studied by Bach (2017) and by E and Wojtowytsch. Its norm is an \(\ell^1\)-type penalty over neurons rather than the \(\ell^2\) RKHS norm, which is exactly what lets it adapt to a small number of important directions and escape the curse of dimensionality that a fixed kernel suffers. The gap between this space and the RKHS is the mathematical measure of what feature learning buys.

Kernels that actually scale

Chapter 13 stopped at classical Nyström and random features. The modern algorithms turned those ideas into methods that run on millions of points in near-linear time, which quietly removed the practical objection that killed kernels in the first place.

FALKON, of Rudi, Carratino, and Rosasco (2017), combines a Nyström subsample with a preconditioned conjugate-gradient solver and provably reaches the optimal statistical rate at a cost close to \(O(n\sqrt{n})\), putting exact-kernel accuracy within reach on datasets that once demanded a deep network. EigenPro, of Ma and Belkin (2017), preconditions stochastic gradient descent with the kernel's top eigenvectors to the same end. On the random-feature side, structured constructions made the features cheap to generate: Fastfood, of Le, Sarlós, and Smola (2013), replaces the dense random projection with a fast Hadamard transform, and orthogonal random features, of Yu et al. (2016), lower the approximation variance for free. Bach (2017) supplied the theory that ties it together, showing random-feature sampling is a quadrature rule whose required number of features is set by the kernel's effective dimension, not by \(n\), which is why a fixed feature budget keeps working as the data grows.

Distributions in the RKHS, matured

The mean embeddings of Chapter 11 grew into a substantial toolkit; Muandet, Fukumizu, Sriperumbudur, and Schölkopf (2017) survey the whole of it. Three developments are worth singling out.

The first is the kernel Stein discrepancy of Chwialkowski, Strathmann, and Gretton (2016) and Liu, Lee, and Jordan (2016). By combining a kernel with Stein's identity it measures how far samples are from a target density using only the target's score, the gradient of its log-density, so it never needs the normalizing constant. That made goodness-of-fit testing for unnormalized models practical and, through Stein variational gradient descent (Liu and Wang, 2016), gave a deterministic particle method for sampling. The second is the conditional mean embedding of Song, Fukumizu, and Gretton (2013), which represents a conditional distribution as an RKHS-valued operator and has become a standard tool in causal inference and reinforcement learning; distribution regression (Szabó et al., 2016) is its supervised cousin, learning a map whose inputs are themselves distributions. The third is the meeting of kernels and optimal transport: the Sinkhorn divergences of Feydy et al. (2019) interpolate between the maximum mean discrepancy and the Wasserstein distance, inheriting the sample efficiency of the former and the geometry of the latter.

Attention is a kernel

The single most consequential modern connection runs to the architecture behind large language models. The attention mechanism of a transformer computes, for each query, a weighted average of values with weights \(\operatorname{softmax}(q^\top k / \sqrt{d})\), and Tsai et al. (2019) observed that this is exactly a kernel smoother: attention is a Nadaraya-Watson estimator whose kernel is the exponential of a scaled inner product. Reading it that way makes the quadratic cost of attention a statement about the kernel, and suggests the same cure the kernel literature already knew.

Linear attention through random features

If the softmax kernel could be written as \(\langle \varphi(q), \varphi(k)\rangle\) for an explicit feature map \(\varphi\), attention would collapse to a running sum and cost linear rather than quadratic time in the sequence length. Katharopoulos et al. (2020) do this with a simple positive feature map, and the Performer of Choromanski et al. (2021) does it with positive random features that provably approximate the softmax kernel, exactly the random Fourier features of Chapter 13 transplanted into a transformer. The scaling wall this book opened with, and the random-feature escape from it, reappear at the heart of modern sequence models.

Kernels on manifolds, graphs, and function spaces

Kernel design, the subject of Part V, gained a principled geometric branch. Borovitskiy et al. (2020, 2021) constructed Matérn Gaussian processes on Riemannian manifolds and on graphs by solving the appropriate heat or Laplace equation, giving well-defined kernels on curved and discrete domains that respect their geometry, a rigorous successor to the diffusion kernels of Chapter 10. In a different direction, the integral-operator view of Chapter 7 was reborn for scientific computing: neural operators, and the Fourier neural operator of Li et al. (2020) in particular, learn maps between function spaces by parameterizing kernel integral operators, so that solving a family of partial differential equations becomes learning a single operator-valued kernel.

The Bayesian face: modern Gaussian processes

Everything in this book has a Bayesian twin, since a kernel is a covariance function and kernel ridge regression is the posterior mean of a Gaussian process. That twin scaled up in parallel. Sparse variational Gaussian processes, of Titsias (2009) and Hensman, Fusi, and Lawrence (2013), summarize the data by a small set of inducing points and fit by variational inference, bringing Gaussian processes to large datasets; deep Gaussian processes, of Damianou and Lawrence (2013), stack them into hierarchies; and deep kernel learning, of Wilson et al. (2016), feeds the output of a neural network into a Gaussian-process kernel, learning the representation and keeping calibrated uncertainty. Where this book treats the kernel as fixed geometry, the Gaussian-process literature treats it as a prior to be learned, and the two views inform each other.

Beyond the RKHS

The frontier is the recognition that the reproducing kernel Hilbert space, for all its power, is a Hilbert space, and some of what modern learning needs lives outside it. Reproducing kernel Banach spaces, of Zhang, Xu, and Zhang (2009), generalize the construction to Banach norms, which can encode sparsity in a way the \(\ell^2\) RKHS norm cannot. The variation-norm spaces of the infinite-width limit are another example, larger than any RKHS and precisely the home of feature learning. The through-line of the modern literature is not that kernels were replaced but that their limits were mapped: we now know, more sharply than the classical theory could say, exactly where the fixed geometry of a kernel is enough, and exactly where a model has to learn its own.

Where to start reading

For the interpolation story, Belkin, Hsu, Ma, and Mandal (2019) and the survey literature that grew around it. For the infinite-width limits, Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler (2018) for the NTK and Chizat and Bach (2018) for the mean-field contrast. For scaling, Rudi, Carratino, and Rosasco (2017). For distribution embeddings, the review of Muandet et al. (2017). For the attention connection, Choromanski et al. (2021). Each opens onto a large and still-active literature, and each connects a modern question back to a chapter of this book.
